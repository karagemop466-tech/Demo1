Price & liquidity

X axis is time, Y axis is the YES contract price in dollars (0–1, a settled YES pays $1.00). Below it, the depth ladder answers “how many contracts are actually available at this price”, and the slider turns that into cost, slippage and fees.

Timestamps must be Unix seconds; millisecond values are rejected with 400 bad_request. The URL builder in assets/kalshi.js refuses those inputs before the request is sent, and unit tests assert it.

Liquidity — what the ladder means

  • Kalshi returns bids only: orderbook_fp.yes_dollars and orderbook_fp.no_dollars, each an ascending array of [price_dollars, count_fp] strings, best bid last.
  • A NO bid at Y is a YES ask at $1.00 − Y. So asks are derived arithmetic, not a separate feed — Orderbook Responses.
  • Empty arrays are ambiguous by design: a closed market, an empty book and a mistyped ticker all return 200 with no rows. Cross-check with GET /markets/{ticker} before concluding a market is dead.
  • Order counts per level are not returned, so “how many orders sit there” is unknowable — only contract quantity. The depth query parameter trims to the best N levels per side.

Cost of taking liquidity

The slider walks the book level by level and reports average fill, slippage against the touch, and the fee under Kalshi's published formula:

fee = roundup(M × 0.07 × C × P × (1 − P)) taker, × 0.0175 maker.

  • Peak cost is at 50¢: $1.75 per 100 contracts. At 1¢ or 99¢ it is $0.07.
  • No settlement fee, so holding to resolution costs nothing extra (fee schedule).
  • KXAAAGASM (the gas series charted here) carries maker multiplier 1 and taker multiplier 1, so quoting it earns no maker rebate.
  • All of this is reproduced by tests, not hand-waved: tests/kalshi.test.mjs asserts 21 rows of the official table.
